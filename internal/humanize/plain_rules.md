Plain language means an average reader of the intended audience understands the text on the first read. It is simple and direct, not simplistic or patronizing. Do not talk down to the reader.

When two rules disagree, the one that makes the text clearer for the reader wins. Shorter is a rule of thumb, not the goal. Clearer writing also beats sounding human: if a change makes the text harder to read in order to sound less like a machine, don't make it.

These rules steer the edit:
- Spend your effort where a reader notices: the main point, the structure, a sentence the reader has to reread. Do the structure first, because a later restructure may cut the sentences you already polished. Leave text that already reads well alone.
- Prefer fewer words. Aim to cut about a fifth of them, and keep the clearer version even when it runs longer. Keep new text as short as the task allows. Count words, not markup. A list must replace words, not add them.
- Protect the words that carry meaning: the ones that define a term, separate two ideas, or prevent a wrong reading. If you cannot cut a word without losing meaning, leave the text as it is and change only what breaks a rule.
- Preserve the message. You may reformat, rephrase, and reorganize the text. You may add a heading, list, or table only where it removes words. Do not change what the original conveys. Keep every fact, name, number, and date as given. Do not add, drop, or alter anything in the original. Keep code, URLs, and quoted text exact.
- Leave out detail the reader doesn't need when you write new text. When you rewrite, keep it, and raise it as a question if it looks out of place.

## Lead with the main point
- In a reply, a report, a web page, or UI text, lead with the main point. Start with the answer and the top task for the reader, then the reasoning, the exceptions, and the details. Say why it matters to the reader.
- Lead with the one thing you most want done. Keep any other requests in the same message, in order, and mark the optional ones. Don't spread them over separate messages: the reader has to reassemble the request, and the thread turns into back-and-forth.
- Write to one audience at a time. When you write to two, give each its own section, and say who "you" and "we" mean.
- On a question-and-answer page, use "I" for the reader and "we" for your side: "How do I apply?", answered by "We review your application."
- Write as if the reader may leave at any point. The first paragraph must stand alone, because many readers stop there. They spend about 5 seconds deciding whether a page is useful, scan the headings and the first words of each line, and read about 18% of it. Every word must earn its place.
- Open with the facts: what the subject is and what matters about it. A comparison, metaphor, or joke can follow the point, not replace it. An image costs the reader a translation step, and they may not take it.
- Give each chunk a heading that says what it holds. Prefer a question, then a statement, then a verb phrase, and keep one kind per level. Start a verb heading with the verb ("Filling out the form"), not "how to". Keep the heading shorter than its content, and skip one-word answers.
- For a longer document, give the bottom line first and keep related material together. If you must send the reader elsewhere, put the cross-reference at the end of the passage, and describe the material well enough for the reader to decide whether to follow.
- Answer the question on the page: keep the main content out of an FAQ or a PDF.

## Write a list or a colon
- Number the items when the order matters, and bullet them when it doesn't. Keep numbered lists out of a sentence ("the facilitator will 1) welcome, 2) introduce"). Don't open items with "firstly" or "secondly": number the list, or link the sentences ("Next, they introduce the speakers").
- Order an unordered list by what matters most to the reader. Alphabetical order is the fallback.
- Use a list for two items or more, and keep it short enough that every item stays next to the lead-in. A single-step procedure is the one exception to the two-item minimum.
- Use a list only where the reader must not miss the items. A list in every paragraph loses its force.
- Use a list for steps and for parallel items. Introduce it with a complete sentence, a fragment every item completes ("Back up these items, such as:"), or a heading that already says what the list holds. Put steps in the order they happen, and move a word every item repeats into the lead-in.
- Say in the lead-in when the list is partial ("such as", "including") or when the reader picks a set ("Answer any 3 of the following").
- Write each step in a procedure as an imperative: "Clone the repository", not "You need to clone the repository".
- In a step, name the place and the goal before the action: "In Google Docs, click File > New", and "To start a new document, click File > New".
- Mark an optional step with "Optional:" as its first word, and point back to a step you gave rather than repeating it.
- Give one way to do the task. Drop "please", "above", "below", and "run the following command", and name what the command does instead: "Deploy the load generator:".
- Use a colon only when the words before it say what follows ("Two kinds:", "The steps are:"). Otherwise write "such as" or "that is" and drop the colon. "The following" works as a noun: "Review the following settings:". Keep every item the same kind. "Sources that back no single sentence, or full titles and dates" mixes a kind of source with a kind of content. The colon rules come from [Google](https://developers.google.com/style/colons) and [Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/punctuation/colons).
- Start the word after a colon lowercase unless it is a proper noun. Don't end a section heading with a colon, and use a full stop in place of a colon before a table, image, or code sample.
- Skip the colon at the end of a UI label, button, or command name. Inside a title, a colon splits the title from its subtitle, and the word after it takes a capital.
- Punctuate items by the type of list, and keep one type per list. A sentence list gives every item a capital letter and a full stop, numbered or bulleted. A fragment list uses lowercase and one full stop after the last item. A stand-alone list, under a heading with no lead-in, uses capitals and no full stops. Put no semicolon or comma at the end of an item, and no "and" or "or" after the second-last one unless the text is legal. A list of single words, code, or link text is the exception: each item takes a capital letter and no full stop. The list rules here come from [Google](https://developers.google.com/style/lists), [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/nceh/clearwriting/writing-tips/2025/bulleted-lists.html), [GOV.UK](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/styles/lists/) and the [Australian Style Manual](https://www.stylemanual.gov.au/structuring-content/lists).
- A description list pairs a term with its meaning, one pair per line. Use one for a glossary or a list of options. Start the term with a capital and no end punctuation, and end the description with a full stop.
- Keep each item to one sentence. In a list you write inside a sentence, use the serial comma: "event logs, clickstream data, and e-commerce transactions".
- Add a table where a rule depends on conditions. An if-then table beats a paragraph of exceptions.
- Nest lists one level deep. Allow a second level where the items genuinely split, and never a third. This depth limit is stricter than the [federal guide](https://github.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/blob/main/_pages/guidelines/organize/use-lists.md), which allows three. Nest headings no more than three.

## Write clear sentences
- Speak to the reader as you, in the present tense, and use contractions where they sound natural.
- In text that speaks for its author, such as a PR, a commit message, or an incident note, the author is a valid subject: "I found the names confusing" is active voice, the same "I" the question-and-answer rule gives the reader. Use it for what the author saw and decided, not for a quality claim on its own: "I think this is better" needs the evidence beside it.
- When you describe a change, name the two states. Text about a change — release notes, a migration guide, a PR, an incident report — has a state before it and one after, and the reader must know which state each sentence describes. Tense alone won't do it: "validates the payload" leaves out when. Label the states, in whatever shape fits the text.
- Prefer short sentences, each with one clear idea, and short paragraphs, each on one topic. Start a paragraph with a sentence that says what it is about.
- Use the active voice and the strongest form of each verb. Make the actor the subject: say we manage the program, not we are responsible for management of the program. Say apply, not make an application. Name the actor in a risk too: say "a manual fix by the infra team", not "manual fixes". Use the passive when the law acts and no person does: "your lease will be terminated".
- Turn hidden verbs back into verbs. A noun ending in -tion, -ment, -sion, or -ance usually hides one: say pay, not make a payment, and review, not conduct a review.
- Unpack noun strings: say developing procedures to protect workers, not underground mine worker safety protection procedures development.
- Keep the subject, verb, and object close together, and put modifiers next to the words they modify. Say you are required to provide only the following, not you are only required to provide the following.
- Count hard words like long words. Acronyms, identifiers, product names, and numbers weigh more than ordinary words, so a short sentence can still be slow to read. When one carries several, split it or move the detail into a list or an example.
- Write the sentence, not a label with a colon. "Blocked: webpack won't build" and "Prod: ruby:3.0.7" leave out the actor and the verb, so the reader has to supply them, and some readers stumble there. Say "Webpack blocks the upgrade." Where a colon would still help, rewrite the sentence first and keep the colon as a fallback.
- Keep the label form for a bold run-in heading in a list ("**Emu**: the best kind of bird") and for a label–value pair in data or UI. Start a run-in heading with a capital and end it with a colon or a period, one choice per list. After a period, start the description with a capital and end it with a period. After a colon, start lowercase, and add a period only when the description has a verb. Don't set a description off with a dash: write "Example: this", not "Example - this". Every other colon needs a complete sentence in front of it, which is what [Google](https://developers.google.com/style/colons), [Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/punctuation/colons) and [Chicago](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/book/ed17/part2/ch06/psec130.html) all say. A fragment lead-in is the one exception, and only when every item completes it.
- Give every pronoun and pointing word (it, this, there) a noun to point at. When it stands for a fact, a claim, or a list, name the thing in words. "Link a source where the sentence uses it" leaves the reader guessing, because both "a source" and "the sentence" fit.
- Put the main clause first and the condition after it: "Complete form 9-123 if you own more than 50 acres and cultivate grapes." Lead with a condition only when it is a few words and a reader would otherwise misread the rule ("Unless you have already submitted an up-to-date resume, you must submit a resume"). When both are long, say the condition is coming and put it in a list after the main clause ("We may hold the hearing at your school if:").
- Use "if" for a condition, and "when" for one that recurs.
- Say what is true rather than what is not. Flag every sentence that says what something is not, then rewrite it in the positive unless the negation is the point. State the rule rather than its exception: write "Each person under 18 must...", not "All persons except those 18 or older must...".
- Count the negatives in a sentence. "Not a labor shortage but jobs that do not pay rent" makes the reader undo two; state the cause, then the fact, as in "the jobs pay too little to cover rent".
- Check a paraphrase the same way. Restating someone else's claim is where a negative slips past a fact check, so put the claim in the positive and read it again.
- Prefer the singular and a person as the subject: say "you must apply", not "individuals and organizations must file applications".
- Use the same term for the same thing throughout. Repeat it rather than reaching for a synonym to sound varied.

## Choose the plain word
- Choose simple, concrete, familiar words. Replace jargon, vague words, and filler with the everyday word: say "buy", not "purchase", and "end", not "terminate". Check each wordy phrase against the plain-word table below.
- Say what you compare against. "Carries more" leaves the reader to guess more than what.
- Give every number a claim. Say what it shows, or cut it: "66 of 265 requests came from curl, so most of the traffic isn't people" earns its figures, while "265 requests, 60 hosts, 66 from curl, 26 from one IP" leaves the reader to work out why.
- Delete openers and hedges: I think, it's worth noting, as you can see, basically, just, really. They usually add nothing, and the point stands without them. Keep a hedge that marks real uncertainty.
- Keep an intensifier that reports the author's own experience: "super confusing" measures how hard the code was to read, while "basically" measures nothing.
- Cut whole sentences and clauses first, then trim words, as in "due to the fact that" becomes "because", "in order to" becomes "to". Do that before you look for a synonym.
- The same cut handles "at this point in time" (now) and "is able to" (can).
- Cut the second word that says the same thing: "due and payable" is "due", "cease and desist" is "stop", and two departments on a "joint project" need no "joint".
- Use must, not shall, for an obligation, must not for a prohibition, may for a choice, and should for a recommendation.
- Write either, or, or both, as the meaning requires, in place of and/or. Rephrase in place of a slash, as in he/she.
- Write out "for example" or "such as", in place of "e.g." or "i.e.". Add a transition word when the reader can't see the link between two sentences, and cut the ones that add nothing.
- Rewrite so a term needs no definition. When a term must stay, define it at first use, unless the reader already knows it. Write out an abbreviation unless it is widely known. Keep each common word in its usual meaning.

## Link the source where it backs the text
- Hyperlink any tool, library, standard, project, product, company, person, or source the reader might not know. Link only to addresses you know.
- Cite your sources. When a fact or quote comes from somewhere else, say where, so the reader can check it, and link the web source when you know its address. Skip this for common knowledge and for your own reasoning. Never make up a source.
- Link each source in the sentence it backs. Add a list at the end only for a source several sentences depend on, or to repeat a linked source with its full title and date.
- Write link text that says where the link leads, in place of "click here" or "read more". The text is a promise about the page behind it, so make the name match that page and be more specific rather than less. Add a short description when the link needs clarification. A screen reader user who moves through the page by its links gets the same promise.

## Use bold sparingly
- Use bold sparingly. All caps reads as shouting, and underlining looks like a broken link.
- In a page, use italics for titles and parenthetical notes such as citations, not for emphasis.

## Design the page for reading
Skip this section for a chat reply. Markdown controls neither type nor layout.
- Design for reading with these settings: ragged right, left-aligned margins rather than justified or centered; leading, the space between lines, two points larger than the type (12 over 14); a serif font for body text and a sans-serif font for headings.
- Limit the document to five or six sections, about two per printed page.
- Make headings bold and left-aligned, in upper and lower case. Triple-space before a heading and double-space after it. Use round or square bullets, at most two styles, and numbers only for a sequence. At the second level, use a dash in place of a hollow bullet. Use shading and borders to accent a chart, not as decoration.
- Use no more than three typefaces, and don't mix fonts within the body.
- Use the built-in list markup, so a screen reader announces the list and its length.
- Align a run-over line with the text, not with the bullet. Add space between items when they run over several lines.
- At the second level of a numbered list, use lowercase letters. Decide once whether numbering carries on across lists, and keep to it.
- When you repurpose print material, cut it by about half and keep only what helps the reader finish a task. The web is a place for action, not narrative.
- Add a picture only when it shows something the words can't.
- Move a list longer than a screenful to its own page or an appendix.
- Check the page for clutter before you finish, such as too many layout devices, odd gaps, and shapes that formed by accident.

## Avoid AI-speak
The rules above serve the reader. These serve how the text sounds, so where one of them fights a rule above, the rule above wins. Keep the headings, the bullets, the short sentences, and the you voice.
- Skip the words that mark machine-written text: delve, tapestry, landscape, robust, leverage, underscore, pivotal, foster, navigate, testament, seamless, myriad. Say the plain word: dig into, area, strong, use, show, key, help, get through, or proof, and cut seamless and myriad outright.
- Cut the opener that announces a point instead of making one, such as "it's important to note", "at its core", "when it comes to".
- Never name an idea with a made-up label such as "the supervision paradox" or "workload creep". Say what happens, in a sentence, and let the reader name it.
- Don't give an opinion a line of its own. "Certainty gets clicks." states a verdict with no fact behind it. Fold it into the sentence that carries the fact, or add the fact.
- Vary the opening of sentences in a paragraph. Three sentences that start "You may" read as a template. Inside a list, repeated openings are fine, because parallel items help the reader compare them.
- Don't stack two lists of three, and don't list what one sentence says more briefly.
- Don't announce the wrap-up with "in summary", "in conclusion", or "to sum up". End on the last point. The transition table allows these words where the reader needs a signpost, which a short piece rarely does.
- Use an em dash no more than once in a piece, and cut emoji, decorative arrows, and title case in headings. A full stop, a comma, or a colon does the dash's job without the habit.

## Check your work
- Check your own work one rule at a time, and loop back over all of them again. A change that fixes one problem can introduce another, so keep going until every change left only trades one quality for another. To settle a tradeoff, rank the qualities: understandability first, fewer words second, simplicity third.
- Before you call a pass done, score the sections above in turn, and quote every violation you still see. Fix what you can fix, and carry the rest as a question.
- Read your text as if you were the reader. For a page or a longer document, also test it with real users. Can they find what they need, understand it, and use it to meet their goals? Ask them to answer a question from it, then to say the content back in their own words.
- Run a keyword sweep. Search the text for not, no, never, without, less and fewer, then reread every hit and rewrite the ones that make their point only by negation.
- Run a label sweep. Search the text for colons, then rewrite every one whose lead-in is a bare label rather than a sentence.
- Run an AI-speak sweep. Search the text for the words listed above, for two sentences in a row that open the same way, and for an em dash, then rewrite each hit that comes from habit rather than from meaning.
- Keep track of what each read covered, so a sentence you skipped shows up instead of slipping through.

## Name the link with a transition word
These words name the link between two sentences. Pick one by the job it does. The [federal guide](https://github.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov/blob/main/_pages/guidelines/organize/use-transition-words.md) gives the same list.

| Job | Words |
| --- | --- |
| Adding a point | also, and, besides, in addition, similarly |
| Giving an example | for example, for instance, for one thing |
| Restating | in other words, that is, in short |
| Showing a result | so, as a result, thus, then |
| Contrasting | but, however, on the other hand, still |
| Summing up | to sum up, in short, in conclusion |
| Sequencing | first, second, then, next, finally |
